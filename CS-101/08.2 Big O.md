We say that $f(x)$ is $O(g(x))$ if there are constants such that
$$\vert f(x)\vert \le C\vert g(x)\vert, \quad x\gt k$$
With $C$ and $k$ being the **witnesses** of $f(x)$ and $O(x)$

To show that a function is big-O of another, we only need one pair of witnesses.

Essentially, after a certain $x$, big O will always be bigger than the full function.

The tendency is to simplify what is found inside of big-O
## For polynomials

We look at the polynomial with the biggest degree, and we put it into Big-O

It is the upper bound of a function.
## Combinations

Because many algorithms have multiple procedures, we have to be able to sum of functions for big-O

>[!Theoreme]
>For two functions that are big-O of their respective function, then their sum is the max of their two associated functions
>$$(f_1 + f_2)(x) = O(g(x)), \quad g(x) = (max(|g_1(x)|, |g_2(x)|)) \iff f_1(x) = O(g_1(x)) \text{ and } f_2(x) = O(g_2(x))$$

This means that for two different functions that are big-O of the same function, then their sum is the equivalent big-O that they had before

>[!Theorem]
>For two functions that are big-O of their respective functions, then their multiplication is the multiplication of both their functions
>$$(f_1\cdot f_2)(x) = O(g_1(x) \cdot g_2(x))$$

## Transitivity

if f(x) is O(g(x)) and g(x) is O(h(x)), then f(x) is O(h(g))
## Exponentials

We can use functions other than just $x^{value}$ to denote a big-O
We can use logarithms, exponentials, factorials, etc...
## Order by Growth

Certain functions grow faster than others.
We check at very far points tho.
## Summary

for a function in reals, we have

| Type         | big-O                   |
| ------------ | ----------------------- |
| Constant     | $O(1)$                  |
| Logarithmic  | $O(\log x)$             |
| Poly-log     | $O((\log x)^d),\;d\gt1$ |
| Linear       | $O(x)$                  |
| Linearithmic | $O(x\log x)$            |
| Polynomial   | $O(x^d),\;d\gt1$        |
| Exponential  | $O(b^x),\;b\gt1$        |
| Factorial    | $O(x!)$                 |

>[!Warning] Important!
>You cannot write $f(x) = O(g(x))$
>You can write $f(x)\in O(g(x))$
